# Article Idea

## Working title
Small systems for keeping personal knowledge useful

## Angle
Explore how simple capture and review habits can turn scattered notes into practical reference material.

---

## Page 1: Core argument

Personal knowledge systems are often described as elaborate tools, but their value usually comes from a few repeatable habits. The first is capture: writing down an idea while it is still fresh, with enough context to understand it later. The second is review: returning to those notes often enough to connect them with current work.

The article could argue that a useful system is less about collecting everything and more about reducing the cost of retrieval. A meeting note should make the next action obvious. A reading note should preserve the idea that was worth remembering. A project note should explain the current state and the next decision.

The piece could open with the familiar experience of finding an old note that contains an important idea but no date, source, or explanation. That small failure illustrates the difference between storage and knowledge. A note becomes useful when its future reader, including the person who wrote it, can quickly understand why it matters.

## Possible sections

- Capture with a sentence of context.
- Separate reference material from temporary reminders.
- Review notes at natural points in existing routines.
- Link related ideas without building a complicated taxonomy.

---

## Page 2: Practical examples

The article could compare three small workflows. After a meeting, record the decision, the owner, and the next checkpoint. After reading, write a short summary and one question the source raises. During a project, keep a running log of decisions so that future changes have a visible reason behind them.

These examples show that a knowledge system does not need to be perfect to be useful. It needs clear conventions that are easy to follow when attention is limited. A small number of note templates can provide enough structure without turning everyday writing into administration.

## Closing idea

The final section could invite readers to run a one-week experiment: capture only notes that support a real task, add one line of context to each note, and spend ten minutes at the end of the week reviewing what was created. The result would be a concrete test of whether the system reduces friction.

## Questions for drafting

- Which note types are most useful in the first month?
- What information makes an old note understandable?
- How can review become part of an existing weekly routine?
- Which kinds of organization create effort without improving retrieval?
